How Much Does Pet Insurance Cost for a Pug by Age? Premiums From Puppy to Senior

Pug pet insurance cost by age follows a curve most owners only notice in hindsight: a policy that feels cheap at the first vaccine visit can cost two or three times as much by the time the muzzle turns gray. Below, we follow that curve year by year using real quotes collected by a pug owner, published insurer examples and owner reports, so you can see the bill coming before it lands.

Quick answer: In November 2025, a pug owner was quoted $56–$81 a month for a 2-year-old pug and $81–$138 a month for a 6-year-old pug (accident and illness, $500 deductible, 90% reimbursement) across five insurers, an average jump of about 61% in four years. Most age-rated plans keep rising after that; in published examples, monthly rates at age 12 ran 155% to 538% above the puppy price. Enrolling young and choosing a pricing model that does not climb with birthdays are the two biggest levers you control.

What does pet insurance cost for a pug at each age?

For a healthy young adult pug, expect roughly $56–$81 per month for a mid-range accident and illness plan; by age six the same coverage was quoted at $81–$138 per month. Puppy prices are usually lower, and senior prices are higher still.

The most useful pug-specific data we found comes from a writer who owns three pugs. In late 2025 she pulled quotes from five well-rated insurers for her own two younger dogs, a 2-year-old and a 6-year-old purebred pug, with identical settings: $500 annual deductible, 90% reimbursement and (for four of the five) a $10,000 annual limit. How a pug's premium compares with the average dog

The North American Pet Health Insurance Association reports that the average US accident and illness premium for dogs was $749.29 a year, or $62.44 a month, at year-end 2024. A young pug sits right around that average. A middle-aged pug is already well above it, and that is before any senior pricing kicks in. Your own quote will move with your ZIP code, deductible and reimbursement level, so treat these numbers as a map rather than a price tag.

Why do pug premiums climb faster than many other dogs' premiums?

Insurers price on expected claims, and pugs claim more: a large UK veterinary-records study found pugs were almost twice as likely as other dogs to be diagnosed with at least one disorder in a year. Age then multiplies that breed risk.

The study came from the Royal Veterinary College's VetCompass program, which compared the records of 4,308 pugs with 21,835 non-pugs over a single year. As Vet Times reported on the RVC pug findings, pugs were 1.9 times as likely to have one or more disorders, had a higher risk for 23 of the 40 most common conditions, and were 54 times more likely to have brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS). Eye ulceration risk was 13 times higher and obesity 3.4 times higher.

Breed rating and age rating stack on top of each other

Think of a pug's premium as two dials. The breed dial is set the day you enroll: a flat face, prominent eyes and skin folds put pugs in a higher-risk pool than a mixed breed of the same size. The age dial turns every year at renewal. Because the health problems pugs are known for (breathing trouble, eye injuries, extra weight on the joints) tend to show up or worsen with time, insurers that rate by age push the second dial harder for breeds like this one. If you want to understand the breathing side in more depth, our pug care guides cover the day-to-day warning signs.

How steep does the price curve get from puppy to senior?

Steep. In published examples, a dog's premium rose from $28 a month as a puppy to $66 at age eight and $119 at age twelve, and across insurers monthly rates grew 155% to 538% between puppyhood and age 12.

There is little public pug-specific data past age six, so the best way to see the senior end of the curve is through consumer research that tracked premiums across a dog's whole life. Checkbook, the nonprofit consumer group, priced the same dogs across 12 insurers and found that most plans start climbing four or five years after enrollment. Its examples, from Philadelphia quotes:

  • Mixed-breed dog with Pets Best: $28/month as a puppy, $66 at age eight, $119 at age twelve, for a total of $8,944 in premiums over almost 13 years.
  • Mixed-breed dog with ASPCA: $67/month as a puppy and $214 at age 12.
  • Beagle with ASPCA: $77/month as a puppy and $247 at age 12.
  • Dogue de Bordeaux with ASPCA: $129/month as a puppy and $415 at age 12.

The beagle is the closest of those to a pug in size, and its premium roughly tripled. Checkbook also notes that some plans charge more than $3,000 a year for a 12-year-old mixed breed, and more for many purebreds. A pug's senior premium will not match any of these exactly, but the shape of the curve is the same: flat-ish for the first few years, then a bend upward that gets sharper every birthday.

The story of one pug, told in renewal notices

Picture Waffles, an illustrative pug enrolled at 10 weeks old on an age-rated plan. Using the quote ratios above, her first renewals barely register. Around her fourth birthday, the letter shows a bigger jump, and by six she costs about 60% more than she did at two. That fits the five-insurer average in the table. At eight, her owner starts reading renewal notices before opening other mail. By twelve, if her plan follows the Checkbook examples above, she costs three to four times her starting price. None of that means Waffles was a bad risk to insure. It means the price was always going to be back-loaded, and the owner who knew that from day one could plan for it.

What do real pug and dog owners say about premiums years later?

Owners who insured early report the same arc: cheap at first, expensive later, and the value depends on whether a big claim arrives. Owners who waited often find the conditions they most need covered are already excluded.

The pug owner behind the quotes above is a cautionary tale for the waiting approach. She never bought insurance for her oldest pug, who at 13 has arthritis, severe skin allergies and a collapsing trachea and has had pneumonia twice. As she explains, most of his treatment would not be covered now because the conditions are pre-existing, which is exactly why she started pricing policies for her two younger pugs.

On the other side of the ledger, in a Tildes discussion about when pet insurance is worth it, one owner who insured two dogs for life said the plans started at "maybe $350/year" and "ultimately they went up to about $1,100/year", adding that they "spent far more on premiums than the policy ever paid out." Another owner in the same thread, whose UK policy started at about £120 a year and renewed at nearly £700, said they had still received far more than they paid in. A third, with a Mastiff-Rottweiler mix, found that conditions "typical for the breed" were excluded, so a knee surgery was not covered.

What those stories have in common

  • Premiums tripled or more over a dog's life in every first-hand account that gave numbers.
  • Whether insurance "won" depended on one or two large claims, not on routine visits.
  • The fine print, meaning pre-existing conditions and breed-typical exclusions, mattered as much as the price. For a pug, ask in writing whether BOAS, stenotic nares and eye ulcers are covered.

Age-rated or enrollment-age pricing: which suits a pug better?

If you plan to keep a policy for your pug's whole life, a plan that prices on enrollment age rather than current age usually rises more slowly. It still rises with vet costs, and it costs more if you enroll an older dog.

Age-rated plans

Most insurers re-price your pug at every renewal based on its current age. They are often the cheapest choice for a puppy or young adult, which is why they dominate comparison sites. The catch is the back half of the curve shown in the calculator: the years when a pug is most likely to claim are the years the premium is highest.

Enrollment-age plans

Checkbook found Trupanion was the only one of the 12 insurers it evaluated whose premium does not rise because a pet has a birthday. Its rate filing states that "your monthly premium will not change due to your pet aging." Two caveats keep this from being a free lunch. The price is set by the age at which you enroll, so a pug enrolled at seven pays more than one enrolled at one. Rates still go up as the cost and use of veterinary care rise; Checkbook's example dog saw a 37% increase in about four years.

Step by step: how to keep your pug's lifetime premium down

Use this checklist the week you bring a pug home, or today if your pug is already older.

  1. Enroll before the first diagnosis. Anything noted in the vet record before coverage starts, including a snorting episode or a scratched eye, can be treated as pre-existing and excluded.
  2. Get same-day quotes from at least four insurers. The table above shows a $57/month gap between the cheapest and priciest quote for the same 6-year-old pug, which is too big to ignore.
  3. Ask each insurer two written questions: "Does my premium rise because my pug ages?" and "Are BOAS surgery, stenotic nares correction and corneal ulcers covered, or excluded as breed-related?"
  4. Run the calculator with each quote and pricing pattern. Compare the total to age 13, not the first-year price.
  5. Choose the deductible deliberately. A higher deductible lowers the premium but shifts routine costs back to you. Set it at an amount you could pay from savings tomorrow without stress.
  6. Diary every renewal. Write down each year's premium. If a jump is far above the pattern you expected, ask the insurer why and re-quote before you accept it.
  7. Keep your pug lean. Obesity risk is 3.4 times higher in pugs. A trim pug is less likely to develop the joint and breathing claims that make insurance expensive in the first place.
  8. Build a side fund anyway. Deductibles, co-insurance and exclusions mean that even insured owners pay part of every big bill.

Common mistakes pug owners make with insurance costs

  • Judging a policy by its first-year price. The cheapest puppy quote can become the most expensive senior policy if it is aggressively age-rated.
  • Waiting "until something happens". By then it is pre-existing. The 13-year-old pug described above is the clearest example.
  • Assuming breathing problems are covered. Some policies exclude conditions typical for the breed. For a pug, that sentence in the policy wording matters more than the premium.
  • Comparing quotes with different settings. A $250 deductible and 90% reimbursement is not comparable to $1,000 and 70%. Lock the settings, then compare.
  • Switching insurers late in life. A new insurer prices your pug at its current age and treats existing conditions as pre-existing, so you lose on both counts.
  • Cancelling in a panic after a big jump. Re-quote first. If your pug is now older and has a medical history, a new policy may cover far less for not much less money.

Pug insurance cost FAQ

How much is pet insurance for a pug puppy?

Public pug-specific puppy quotes are scarce. For context, Checkbook's examples started at $28–$77 a month for a mixed breed and a beagle as puppies, and a 2-year-old pug was quoted $56–$81 a month in November 2025. Puppy quotes are often lower than young-adult quotes on age-rated plans, but get real quotes for your ZIP code.

How much does insurance cost for a 6-year-old pug?

One owner's November 2025 quotes for a 6-year-old pug ranged from $80.56 to $137.75 a month with a $500 deductible and 90% reimbursement, an average of about $106 a month.

Can you still insure a senior pug?

Many insurers will quote older dogs, but the premium is priced for the higher age and pre-existing conditions are not covered. For a senior pug that already has breathing, eye or joint problems, much of the likely treatment may be excluded.

Why are pugs more expensive to insure than many breeds?

Pugs claim more often. The RVC VetCompass study found pugs were 1.9 times as likely to have at least one disorder in a year and 54 times more likely to have BOAS than other dogs, and insurers price that risk into the breed rating.

Does any pet insurance not raise premiums as a pug ages?

Checkbook found Trupanion was the only one of 12 insurers it evaluated that does not raise premiums because a pet gets older. It prices on enrollment age, and its rates still rise with veterinary costs.

Is pet insurance worth it for a pug?

It depends on whether you could pay a large surprise bill from savings. Use the calculator above: if the average yearly vet spending needed to break even looks unlikely for your pug, a dedicated savings fund may serve you better. If one large BOAS or eye bill would be a crisis, insuring young is the safer bet.

The bottom line on pug insurance costs by age

Pug insurance costs by age follow a predictable story: modest at the start, about 60% higher by six, and often three times the starting price or more by the senior years on age-rated plans. You can't stop that arc, but you can decide how it ends. Enroll before the first diagnosis, compare the total to age 13 instead of the first-year price, ask in writing about breathing and eye coverage, and keep a side fund for what no policy pays.
