Getting a Second Pug: What Owners Say Changed, What Went Wrong and How They Introduced Them

Getting a second pug can give your first dog company and change how you handle meals, naps and time on the couch. Before you decide, plan for a quiet first meeting with one handler per dog and set up places where they can eat and rest separately. Once they meet, watch whether both dogs can look away, move off and settle. Those are better things to track than how quickly they curl up together.

What owners noticed once there were two pugs

In Chris and Martin’s account of adopting Troy, Troy settled in quickly with Archie, their resident pug, and a visiting Cockapoo. The fosterer brought Troy to their home and shared what she had learned about him. Troy joined Archie’s existing bedtime routine on the owners’ bed, but used dog beds in the kitchen and office during the day. Archie did little playing. The owners still described him as company for Troy.

In a different household, Sunny and Rosy’s owner describes Rosy repeatedly going after Sunny’s back legs. Sunny sometimes put up with it and sometimes signaled she’d had enough. Their owner also describes Sunny climbing over Rosy to get a spot beside her on the couch. For an introduction plan, the question that story raises is whether the quieter dog can end the interaction. Set aside a place to nap where the other pug can’t follow.

These are individual accounts. One describes an adult pug fitting into an existing routine and the other shows how differently two pugs can want to interact at the same moment. Think about which parts of your current routine you’d like to keep available to your resident pug, including time alone with you.

How to introduce a second pug on neutral ground

Ask the rescue or current owner whether you can arrange a meeting before you commit. Pick a quiet open route with room to veer away and a handler for each dog. Dogs Trust advises beginning with distance and loose leads, gradually moving closer if both dogs are comfortable. Let them sniff briefly if both remain relaxed, then walk on. You can finish the outing after a calm walk at a distance and arrange another visit.

Agree on a simple way to call for space before you start, such as “let’s widen out.” If your first pug has trouble moving past unfamiliar dogs, use the early cues in our guide to pug leash reactivity to decide how much room you want for the walk. Check each dog’s gear before you meet, using the standing and moving fit checks in our pug harness guide.

What a good walk can’t tell you about sharing a home

At home the dogs encounter things that weren’t on the walk, such as a favorite bed, a bowl on the floor or one person they both want to sit beside. Battersea recommends picking up food and toys before bringing the new dog inside and providing separate beds and multiple water bowls. Start in a roomy area where each can explore and you can guide them apart. If the entry opens onto a tight hall, arrange for one handler to take their pug to a separate room before the other comes through.

Decide where each pug will eat before the first meal. Use separate spaces and pick up the bowls before reopening the door. If you’re choosing a second bowl, our flat-face feeder comparison shows how different bowl shapes fit into a feeding station. Keep favorite chews for private breaks while you learn how the dogs behave around one another.

Make the new routine easy to repeat on an ordinary morning. Work out who takes which dog out, where the other waits, and whether you can manage a separate trip if each pug has different needs. Keep your first dog’s familiar route when possible. Our bad-weather potty routine guide can help you plan a short fallback outing while the new pug learns where to go.

What to change when one pug keeps crowding the other

Watch the moment a pug tries to end an interaction. If one turns away from the couch and the other follows, call the follower away and give each dog a break. If the same thing happens every time you sit down, try spending time with each in a separate room before trying another shared session. Look for a return to ordinary behavior afterward, such as exploring the room or choosing a bed.

Use a closed door for breaks if the dogs keep bothering each other through a gate. Supervise early shared time, including quiet moments when one is about to settle. If you can’t supervise, give each their own space. If there’s lunging or snapping, end the session, keep them apart and ask a qualified behavior professional or the rescue’s behavior team to help you plan the next step.

Questions to ask before getting a second pug

Should I get a puppy or an adult pug as my second dog?

Ask how the candidate plays, handles breaks and responds when another dog moves away. Then ask whether a supervised meeting is possible. Think about how much separate training and potty time you can offer, particularly if the new dog is still learning the household routine.

Can I introduce the dogs in my living room?

Start outdoors on neutral ground when possible. If the walk goes well, use a roomy area at home and take up food and toys first. Give each pug a place to go if they want a break, and plan how you’ll move them apart before they come inside.

Do I need to feed two pugs separately?

Start with separate feeding spaces. Give each dog time to finish in peace, then pick up the bowls before bringing them back together. Keep that routine if it works for your household. There’s no reason to make access to a shared bowl a test of how well they get along.

How long does it take for two pugs to get along?

Go by what both dogs can do in the current setup. Look for easy disengagement, undisturbed rest and each dog being able to move around the room. If one is still turning away or being followed, make the next session easier and give them separate breaks.

What should I ask the rescue before bringing a second pug home?

Ask what the fosterer has seen at meals, around resting places and during play with other dogs. Find out who can help if the introduction gets difficult. Discuss a neutral meeting and be ready to describe how you’ll give both pugs quiet time in your home.
