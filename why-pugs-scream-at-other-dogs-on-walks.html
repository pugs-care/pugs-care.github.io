Why Does My Pug Scream at Other Dogs on Walks?

A pug that squeals, barks or lunges as another dog comes into view may be showing leash reactivity. Your pug may want the other dog farther away, may be frustrated about being unable to greet it, or may be too excited to pass calmly. The most useful clue is what happens before the noise: can your pug glance away and move with you, or do they plant their feet and stare? When you see that stare, make room early by crossing where you can, stepping aside or turning back.

Why the same scream can mean different things

The leash limits where your pug can go. A dog that wants to retreat may brace, lean away or try to duck behind you. A dog eager to approach may lean forward and whine as the gap closes. A dog can also pull toward another dog and then hesitate when it gets close. That mix is one reason to give your pug room to move away and watch how their behavior changes. Dogs Trust describes both frustration and worry as possible drivers and recommends looking at changes in posture and expression as the trigger appears.

In one pug owner discussion about screaming around dogs, the poster described a pug who asked to play loudly and then sometimes seemed nervous. Another owner described using treats and a familiar attention cue while the other dog passed. These reports show what owners notice and try. They leave open what any individual pug is feeling and how they will respond to training.

Think about what the setting asks of your pug. Meeting a familiar dog in an open space and passing an unfamiliar one at the mouth of a narrow driveway are different tasks. On your next walk, notice whether your pug reacts most to dogs coming straight toward them, dogs standing still, or dogs that suddenly appear. Use the answer to choose where you walk and where you turn.

What to do before, during and after the scream

Watch the leash and your pug's feet as well as their face. A dog can be silent while staring with their weight pitched forward. If you can spot that change, you have more time to choose an open route before both dogs arrive at the same spot.

On a narrow sidewalk, you can say “Please give us some room” as you turn. If there is a side path or a clear spot behind a hedge, use it before the other dog reaches you. Trainers at Karen Pryor Clicker Training suggest using a visual barrier when you cannot get more distance. Pick a spot with room to come out again after the dog passes.

How to practice a turn your pug can follow

Pick a short cue such as “this way.” Indoors or in a quiet outdoor spot, say it once, make a smooth turn and reward your pug near your leg when they follow. Set up the first attempts where the leash can hang loose. Once the cue is familiar at home, try it in a familiar outdoor spot without another dog bearing down on you. Practicing on both sides gives you options when one direction is blocked.

When a dog appears ahead, start moving while your pug can still turn with you. If they keep staring, use your available exit and save the cue practice for a quieter stretch. Repeating the cue while you stand in place leaves the same narrow gap and the same approaching dog. The turn is a way to get room, and you can practice the training exercise later.

Keep leash handling simple. Check that your pug can walk and turn comfortably in their usual gear before heading to a busier route. Our pug harness fit guide illustrates the fit checks you can do at home. For reward timing, start with a small piece your dog normally takes easily. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior recommends reward-based training and advises against leash corrections and other aversive methods.

How to find a workable distance for practice

Choose a spot with a clear view and a way out, such as the far edge of a broad path. When another dog appears, watch your pug. If they glance, turn back or continue walking with a loose body, reward the check-in and keep the gap. If they freeze or load the leash, back away or leave. Where that change happens is a useful way to think about your pug's threshold in that setting.

A pug might take food while still staring, then lunge as soon as the food is gone. Use food alongside other observations, including how readily your pug turns their body and whether they can resume sniffing. If you find yourself holding a treat at their nose for the whole pass, move farther away next time and see what happens when your pug has a choice to turn back to you.

Compare similar walks when you can. A dog walking away in an open field presents a different picture from a barking dog coming around a corner. On wet days, a familiar bad-weather potty route can give you a short outing without the pressure to complete the whole loop. If you use a jacket, try it indoors first and use the pug clothing measurement guide to check how it sits while your dog walks and turns.

When to skip greetings and end practice

You can walk past dogs without introducing them. If a handler asks to say hello, “We're going to keep moving today” gives them time to hold their dog back. If your pug starts pulling or squealing, use the distance you have. Save any planned introduction for a separate outing with a familiar dog, willing handlers and enough space for both dogs to move away.

Apartment entrances can make this difficult. Pause where you can see the exit before entering a shared hall or leaving the building. If a dog is coming through and you have a clear retreat, go back to your own doorway or wait in an open area until they pass. For a route with several blind corners, pick in advance which corner you can avoid entirely. That decision is easier at home than with your dog already at the end of the leash.

If your pug spends the rest of the outing watching for the next dog, cut practice short. Head to a quieter spot or go home by the simplest route. If walks regularly leave you unable to maintain space, or you are concerned about contact with another dog, a reward-based behavior professional can observe your route and help you set up a manageable practice session.

What progress looks like on ordinary walks

After a pass, write down the place, the other dog's direction, your pug's first movement and what they did after you made room. Landmarks are enough. “Across the street at the blue mailbox” will help you reproduce the setup without estimating a precise distance. Add what you tried, such as turning away before the corner, so you can tell which part of the walk you changed.

Here is a sample entry: “Dog came straight toward us at the alley. Pug stopped and stared. We turned back behind the hedge. Pug sniffed the verge on the way home.” The next time, turn before reaching the alley or start on the wider side of the block. If your pug can keep moving and check in there, repeat that setup before making the pass harder.

A quiet walk can mean you chose a better route; a successful practice pass can mean your pug saw another dog and still had room to disengage. Track both. A shorter stare, a turn you can make together, or a return to sniffing after the pass gives you a more specific measure than volume alone. For pug leash reactivity, the goal can simply be a walk where you both have room to keep going.

Common questions about pug leash reactivity

Why does my pug scream even though they like other dogs?

A dog that enjoys familiar playmates can still get frustrated by a leash or feel uneasy about a head-on pass. Watch what changes as the other dog approaches and make enough space for your pug to turn or move on.

What if my pug takes treats and then starts barking?

Check whether their body stayed stiff or their gaze stayed fixed while they ate. On the next pass, start farther away and watch for an easy turn of their head or a return to sniffing as well as interest in food.

Should I ask my pug to sit when another dog passes?

If your pug is already staring or stiffening, give them space to move away. A sit can work in a roomy spot if your pug can settle there and the other dog has room to pass.

How far away should I start?

Start where your pug can notice the other dog and still look away or move with you. Use a landmark you can remember and allow more room if the next dog is moving directly toward you or the path is narrower.

What if my pug only reacts when I walk both dogs together?

Try a quiet walk with each dog separately and note where each first stiffens or starts watching the other dog. Use those walks to practice your turn with one leash at a time before deciding which routes to try together.

How long will it take for walks to get quieter?

Measure the next few walks by what your pug can do in comparable situations, such as looking back at you or sniffing after a dog passes. Start with a route and distance where those behaviors are possible and practice there before closing the gap.
